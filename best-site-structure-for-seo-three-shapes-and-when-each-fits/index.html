Best Site Structure for SEO: Three Shapes and When Each Fits

The best site structure for SEO is the shallowest tree that still gives each need people search for its own page, with every important page a few clicks from home. Which shape that tree takes depends on what you sell: one product, many kinds of items, or one service in many towns.

Below are the three shapes most small and mid-size sites end up with. Each is drawn for one invented business, with the one rule that keeps it healthy.

Pick your shape

If your site mostly... Shape Drawn below for
explains one product in a handful of pages Flat a software maker
sells many items that fall into groups Hub-and-spoke a shop
offers the same service in several towns By location a roofer

1. Flat: a software maker

Say a small company sells one invoicing app. Its site needs about eight pages, and all of them can sit right under the home page.

Home
├── Features
├── Pricing
├── Integrations
├── Security
├── Customers
├── Help
└── Contact

Every page is one click from home, so nothing gets buried. The shape breaks later, when blog posts and help articles pile up at the top level with no grouping.

The rule: once more than about a dozen pages share one level, give them a parent. Twelve help articles become one Help section, not twelve top-level pages.

2. Hub-and-spoke: a shop

Say an online shop sells kitchen knives and the things that go with them. Each hub covers a broad need and links down to narrower pages. Each narrower page links back up and across to its siblings.

Home
├── Chef's knives            <- hub
│   ├── Japanese chef's knives
│   ├── German chef's knives
│   └── Knives for small hands
├── Sharpening               <- hub
│   ├── Whetstones
│   ├── Honing rods
│   └── How to sharpen a knife
└── Storage                  <- hub
    ├── Magnetic strips
    ├── Knife blocks
    └── Blade guards

A hub is not a bare list of links. It answers the broad search ("best chef's knife") on its own, then points to the pages that answer the narrower ones.

The rule: a hub earns its place only with at least three pages under it. Two pages and a hub make a menu, not a section.

3. By location: a roofer

Say a roofing company in Denver also works in Aurora, Lakewood and Boulder. People search by job and by town, so the tree needs both.

Home
├── Roof repair
├── Roof replacement
├── Storm damage
└── Areas we serve
    ├── Denver
    ├── Aurora
    ├── Lakewood
    └── Boulder

Each town page names the work done there and the roofing issues common in that town (hail, ice dams, old cedar shakes), then links to the matching job pages. The job pages link back to the towns.

The rule: a town page needs something true about that town. If the only difference between the Aurora and Lakewood pages is the name, keep one page and list both towns on it.

Checks that apply to all three

  • Depth. Any page you want found sits no more than three clicks from home.
  • One page, one need. Two pages that answer the same search compete with each other. Merge them.
  • Readable URLs. /sharpening/whetstones/ says what the page is. /cat-7/item-212/ says nothing.
  • Links go up and across. Each page links to its parent and to its closest neighbours, not to everything.
  • Leave things out on purpose. A need too small for its own page becomes a section of a page that exists.
  • Redirect on rename. Moving a page without a redirect breaks each link that pointed to it.

The shape is the last decision

Draw the tree after the list of needs, not before. Write down what people actually search for, put the searches that one page can answer together, and see which groups are big enough to become a section. The shape follows from that list.

A tree drawn from the company's departments shows how the business is run. Visitors arrive with a need, not a department, so the tree should start from the need.

Quick answers

Is a flat structure better for SEO? Only while the site is small. Past a dozen or so pages, groups help readers and keep each page's topic clear.

How deep should a site be? Home, section, page is enough for most small businesses. Large stores go one level deeper. Few sites need more.

Should each product category get a hub? Only the ones with enough pages under them and a broad search of their own.
